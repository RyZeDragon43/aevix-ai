THE BIG IDEA

A data center is still a data center—even in orbit.

AI chips process requests and model workloads. Solar arrays supply power, radiators release waste heat, and communications equipment connects the system with users and ground networks.

Space offers sunlight and new deployment possibilities, but it is not automatically cheaper, colder, or faster than computing on Earth.

How the system would work

Six systems. One orbital network.

01 / POWER

☀ Solar electricity

Deployable solar panels generate electricity. Batteries and power electronics keep the computers running when the spacecraft passes through Earth's shadow.

02 / COMPUTE

▦ AI processing

Radiation-tolerant spacecraft systems would host AI accelerators, memory, and storage. NVIDIA hardware is a potential technology direction, not an agreed partnership.

03 / COOLING

❄ Thermal control

Computer chips produce substantial heat. A closed-loop cooling system would move that heat to large radiators, which emit infrared energy into space. Vacuum prevents ordinary air cooling.

04 / CONNECTIVITY

⌁ Ground connections

Radio or optical communications links would send workloads and results between orbital hardware and Earth. Latency, bandwidth, ground-station coverage, and weather affect performance.

05 / PROTECTION

◇ Space resilience

Shielding, error correction, redundancy, and remote recovery would help defend against radiation, component failures, debris risks, and difficult in-orbit maintenance.

06 / DEPLOYMENT

↗ Launch and expansion

Start with a small demonstration payload, test real power and cooling performance, then consider modular expansion. SpaceX is a prospective launch partner only.

Research milestones

Our proposed path to orbit

  1. Phase 1 — Earth first: Launch the Aevix assistant and test terrestrial data-center economics.
  2. Phase 2 — Feasibility: Model launch mass, power, cooling radiator size, networking, and cost per AI workload.
  3. Phase 3 — Demonstration: Seek partners for a small orbital computing experiment.
  4. Phase 4 — Scale only if proven: Expand if performance, reliability, sustainability, and economics justify it.

Engineering realities

Heat: Space is a vacuum; heat must be radiated away, often requiring large surfaces.

Energy: Solar panels and batteries add mass, and eclipse periods reduce power generation.

Cost: Launch, replacement, insurance, debris mitigation, and end-of-life disposal could outweigh benefits.

Network: Some real-time tasks may work better in Earth-based data centers.

Aevix Space Division is a proposed concept, not an operating orbital service. No SpaceX, NVIDIA, or Tesla partnerships have been established.
